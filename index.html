    Observation 1

    Student A comes back tired from a long day of work and wants to cook in the kitchen but does not feel like socializing. His roommate sees him in the kitchen, and starts chatting. Student A doesn’t want to admit that he’s not in the mood to socialize because he is technically in a common area and doesn’t want his roommate to feel rejected, even though his need for space isn’t anything personal against.

    Observation 2

    Student B has a midterm in two days and is feeling stressed because he knows nothing about the class and needs to catch up on it. He decides to grind in the common study area for a change of scenery. His roommate who is also studying there starts showing him funny jokes on his phone. Student B tries to explain that he is studying for a midterm, but his roommate is not feeling the sense of urgency because the exam is two days away. Student B feels like his boundaries are not being respected because unfortunately his roommate is not getting social cues and he doesn’t want to be blunt and offend him in any way.

    Observation 3

    Student C is studying in the common study space because he is doing light work and feels like socializing; he would enjoy being interrupted for short breaks. His roommate passes by and avoids acknowledging him and greeting him because he assumes that Student C is locked in and does not want to be interrupted from his work. Student C feels overlooked and a bit disappointed, since he was open to interaction but his openness went unnoticed. He also thinks that his roommate lacks manners since he didn’t know his intentions of not interrupting his work.

    Observation 4

    Student D comes back tired from a long lab and sits in the living room to rest. Their roommate, assuming they are available, initiates a casual conversation. Student D, drained and not in the mood to engage, responds briefly. The roommate interprets this reaction as rudeness, even though Student D simply needs downtime. The lack of an easy way to communicate this boundary creates tension, as Student D feels guilty for seeming unfriendly while the roommate feels brushed off.

    Identifying the Problem

    In shared student housing, roommates often misinterpret social cues about availability for interaction. When students are tired, stressed, or simply seeking alone time, they may avoid directly expressing their needs to prevent seeming rude or unfriendly. Conversely, when students are open to socializing, their willingness is often overlooked because others assume they are busy.

    In an ideal world, clear communication and acknowledging that not everything is to be taken personally, would easily solve these problems. Unfortunately, students have a difficult time openly stating their boundaries without fearing the other person's reaction, or even starting conversations in fear of getting dismissed. All of this can lead to frustration, awkwardness, bad tension and missed opportunities for connection.

    The main identified problem is that students lack a subtle, non‑verbal way to communicate mood and boundaries in shared spaces.

    Therefore, our proposed solution is NOORing, a coaster that communicates emotional availability through color code:

    • Green = available or open to socialize
    • Orange = semi‑focused or prefer some distance
    • Red = not available or no socializing

    This color‑coded system provides an intuitive, at‑a‑glance understanding of availability without requiring verbal negotiation or app‑based communication.

    To demonstrate this interaction, we filmed three different ways the same scenario can go. In the video without NOORing, there was misunderstanding of each other’s intentions and some comments were definitely misinterpreted as rudeness. In the other videos using the NOORing, we can clearly see that messages were clearly received and no one got offended.

    Developing Personas

    From observations 1 and 4, we conclude that students often come back tired after class or labs, and being in a common space makes them feel pressured to socialize, even when they don’t want to. They avoid directness to not seem rude. We can notice a common pattern: students who need privacy and rest but feel guilty or misunderstood when setting boundaries.

    From observation 2, we see that students under stress (especially before big exams) feel that their need for focus isn’t being respected. They already feel overwhelmed and hesitate to be blunt, so distractions continue. We can notice a common pattern: students who need to be productive but struggle to communicate urgency without offending.

    From observation 3, we can identify students in a light‑work or relaxed mood who may welcome interaction, but others mistakenly assume they are busy. This leaves them feeling ignored. We can notice a common pattern: students who are socially open but whose openness goes unnoticed, leading to missed connections.

    The above analysis helped us formulate 3 personas who will benefit from the NOORing coaster:

    Persona 1: Alex (20) – Engineering

    He lives in a dorm with 3 roommates. His challenging major often leaves him drained after long days of classes and assignments. He values downtime but doesn’t want to seem rude to his roommates.

    Benefit: NOORing allows roommates to understand his need for space without taking it personally.

    Persona 2: Henry (21) – Psychology

    He lives in a shared student housing with 2 other students. Anxiety spikes around exams and deadlines; he needs concentration in common spaces.

    Benefit: Calm, respectful way to signal focus and avoid distractions without confrontation.

    Persona 3: Stella (19) – Political Science

    She lives in a dorm with 2 roommates, loves bonding but fears intruding.

    Benefit: Subtle, non‑verbal cues about roommates’ availability so she can socialize confidently.

    Illustrating a Use‑Case Scenario

    Henry, a 21‑year‑old psychology student, has a midterm in two days. He chooses the common study space for a change of environment but needs to focus, so he brings NOORing.

    User Action

    Before starting, Henry taps the button twice to set the ring to red (not available).

    System Reaction

    NOORing glows softly red; a roommate notices and refrains from interrupting.

    Break

    After two hours, Henry taps once to switch to green. A roommate invites him for a short walk; Henry accepts.

    Outcome: reduced tension, preserved productivity, and better‑timed social breaks.

    Finding Related Products

    Existing products use lighting/color in drinkware (e.g., temperature indication) or for visual effects/branding, but they don’t directly address mood signaling in shared living.

    Moodsignal (laptop‑attached LED) changes colors via a website. Luxafor targets corporate use and is expensive (~€149). These devices are separate objects, rely on apps/sites, and are less suited to casual roommate settings.

    Students sometimes use makeshift tools (e.g., a desk whiteboard) to communicate availability, but these aren’t portable or habit‑forming and add mental load. NOORing integrates signaling into an everyday coaster, aligning with calm‑technology principles.

    Comparing the Products

    Alex’s situation: Moodsignal adds cognitive burden when Alex is exhausted. NOORing is a quick tap, no screen required; it communicates intentional emotional states rather than decorative effects.

    Henry’s situation: Corporate devices like Luxafor are stationary; Henry moves between rooms. NOORing travels with him and provides three simple states (green/orange/red) without opening a website.

    Stella’s situation: Laptop LEDs aren’t visible in other rooms or when the laptop is closed. NOORing is visible across shared spaces and is student‑affordable (~$25–35 target).

    Why NOORing wins: portable, simple, affordable, and built for boundary setting—not just aesthetics.

    System Flow

    The user places their cup on NOORing and taps the capacitive sensor (1 = green/available, 2 = orange/moderate focus, 3 = red/do not disturb). The Arduino processes the tap count (Touch Handler), updates the mood state (State Manager), and triggers the LEDs (LED Controller). Power Manager sleeps after 30 minutes of inactivity.

    Component‑by‑Component Breakdown

    Hardware Assembly (8 hours/member)

    • Hours 1–2: Order/receive pre‑existing components (most available or ship within ~1 week).
    • Hours 3–5: Breadboard prototype: connect Arduino → NeoPixel ring, capacitive sensor, power system using Adafruit tutorials.
    • Hours 6–8: Solder final circuit on perfboard; test functionality.
    • Risk mitigation: Well‑documented components; ESP32 as drop‑in if Nano unavailable.

    Firmware Development (10 hours/member)

    • Hours 1–3: Implement three‑state FSM in Arduino IDE.
    • Hours 4–6: Tap detection using CapacitiveSensor examples.
    • Hours 7–9: Integrate Adafruit_NeoPixel; map colors (green/orange/red).
    • Hour 10: Add sleep after timeout.
    • Risk mitigation: Libraries are documented; if capacitive sensing is noisy, swap to a simple button (~2h change).

    Enclosure Design (4 hours/member)

    • Hours 1–2: CAD modeling in TinkerCAD: two‑piece coaster (base + top) with diffuser ring.
    • Hours 3–4: 3D print at makerspace or laser‑cut acrylic.
    • Risk mitigation: If printing fails, switch to flat laser‑cut design (~1h redesign).

    User Testing & Iteration (6 hours/member)

    • Hours 1–2: Recruit 6 participant pairs (REB‑approved protocol).
    • Hours 3–4: Usage sessions with 3 roommate pairs over 2 weeks.
    • Hours 5–6: Debrief interviews; tune LED brightness/colors.
    • Risk mitigation: Standard HCI protocols; MVP test feasible with 3 pairs.

    Total Estimated Time: 28 hours/member (within 20–30h guideline). Feasibility Confidence: HIGH — no advanced fabrication; ~300 lines of C++ using documented libs.

    Title of Project NOORing: Ambient Availability Signaling for Shared Spaces
    Project Description This study evaluates the NOORing smart coaster prototype, which uses ambient LED lighting (green = available, orange = moderately busy, red = do not disturb) to facilitate non‑verbal boundary communication between roommates in shared student housing. Participants will use the NOORing device in their natural living environments over a 7‑day trial period, followed by semi‑structured interviews examining its impact on roommate communication patterns, boundary‑setting comfort, and interpersonal tension levels. The study aims to assess whether calm technology can reduce social friction and improve quality of life in shared spaces.
    Number and Type of Participants 6–12 university students (ages 18–25, all genders) currently living in shared housing with 1–3 roommates. Participants must regularly use common spaces (kitchen, living room, or study areas) for daily activities. Recruitment targets a minimum of 3 roommate pairs (6 individuals total) to ensure adequate data collection across diverse living situations. Participants should have no prior exposure to mood‑signaling devices to ensure unbiased feedback.
    How and from where are they recruited Recruitment occurs via:
    • In‑class announcements
    • Student housing group chats
    • Personal contacts (family, friends)
    Materials emphasize voluntary participation and time commitment (30‑minute briefing; 7‑day usage with no active effort; 30‑minute debrief). Participants may withdraw anytime without penalty. Interested students complete a brief screening questionnaire to confirm eligibility.
    What risk could this study entail for participants? Minimal Psychological Risks:
    • Discomfort explicitly signaling unavailability (red light may feel antisocial or rude).
    • Interpersonal tension if the red state is used frequently, leading to feelings of rejection.
    • Social pressure to use the device or to respect signals perceived as rigid.
    • Research‑related anxiety from being observed/evaluated.
    No Physical Risks: Device operates at low voltage (3.7–5V) with safety‑certified components and no exposed electrical elements.
    How can these risks be mitigated?
    • Comprehensive Pre‑Study Briefing (30 min): Position NOORing as a facilitator of communication; encourage participants to explain its purpose to roommates.
    • Informed Consent & Withdrawal Rights: Emphasize voluntary participation and unconditional right to withdraw at any stage without penalty or academic impact.
    • Mid‑Study Check‑In Protocol: Brief text‑based check‑ins on day 3 to monitor tension, discomfort, or technical issues.
    • Post‑Study Debriefing Session: 30‑minute semi‑structured interview for candid feedback.
    • Technical Reliability Testing: 48‑hour stress testing of all devices before deployment.
    • Data Protection & Anonymity: Anonymize all collected data; exclude personally identifiable information from presentations.
    • Roommate Inclusivity: Encourage briefing of roommates about the study’s nature even if only one roommate participates formally.
